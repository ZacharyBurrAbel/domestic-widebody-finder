Why cargo schedules give it away

Airlines sell cargo space in the belly of ordinary passenger planes. Freight forwarders need to know which planes fly where, because a widebody holds far more cargo than a narrowbody. So the airlines publish it — publicly, for free, with dates and flight numbers.

That cargo schedule is really a widebody schedule. A widebody flying inside the US means lie-flat beds up front on a route where you'd normally get a recliner. Everything in the search tab came out of the three documents below.

The one trap in all this

Alaska and Hawaiian publish their freighters and their passenger widebodies in the same document, and the freighter section comes first. Those flights have no seats at all — they're cargo-only 737s and A330s running freight around Alaska and the Pacific. You cannot book one.

Tell them apart by the subfleet code. Anything ending in F is a freighter: 7GF and 8GF are the converted 737s, and Hawaiian's A330 freighters follow the same pattern. Flight numbers in the 7000s are another giveaway. The passenger widebodies are the 787-9 and A330-200 rows further down.

This is the one place the whole cheat code breaks. It works because passenger planes sell the space under the cabin, so the freight schedule doubles as a widebody passenger schedule. A dedicated freighter schedule is the opposite — real cargo, no cabin.

Reading one without getting lost

  1. Find the domestic section

    These documents are mostly international freight. Skip past it. American and Delta label their domestic widebody tables outright. United's sits about a third of the way in under Domestic U.S.

  2. Look at the aircraft column

    Short codes: 772, 77W, 788, 789, 767, A330, A350. Those are aircraft types, and the plane decoder tab translates every one into what seat you'd actually be sitting in.

  3. Read the frequency and the date range together

    The part people skip, and it's the whole game. A row reading 1234567 from 10/5 to 10/24 means daily, but only inside that window. A row reading just 6 means Saturdays only. Outside the window you get a narrowbody.

  4. Search that route on those dates

    Nothing special — book it like any other ticket. Pull up the exact dates and compare fares day by day.

  5. Confirm on the seat map before you pay

    Open seat selection. A 1-2-1 or 2-2-2 layout with three or four cabins means you've got the widebody. If it's 3-3 behind two rows of recliners, the schedule moved. Ten seconds, and it's the check that matters most.

What the schedule won't tell you

Cargo schedules only list widebodies, so the narrowbodies carrying real flat beds never appear in them: American's A321T and A321XLR on the transcon routes, and the Delta 757s with sixteen Delta One beds. Those are in the search tab anyway, tracked by hand and marked Not in cargo schedules so you know where they came from.

United also publishes one aircraft family rather than the exact variant, so its rows read 777 or 787 where American splits out 772 and 788. Either way it's Polaris.

And schedules get revised mid-month without notice. An aircraft swap on the day beats anything printed weeks earlier. Treat every row here as a strong lead, never a guarantee.

Three things nobody tells you

Business class is sold as domestic first

On a domestic widebody, the international business cabin is what the airline sells you as first class. You buy a domestic first-class ticket and get an international bed. That's the entire arbitrage.

Premium economy is usually sold as coach

The underrated play. On most domestic widebody flights the premium economy cabin isn't sold separately — it's released as extra-legroom economy. A regular coach ticket plus a seat fee can land you in Premium Plus or Premium Select. Hawaii routes are the exception, where it's often sold as its own cabin.

Even the back is better

Wider cabin, more bathrooms, better air, quieter ride, real seatback screens. If the widebody prices the same in economy — and it often does — take it regardless of what's up front.

There's no secret booking channel

This comes up every time, so let's kill it: you book these exactly like any other flight. Airline website, online travel agency, points, whatever you normally use. The cargo schedule isn't a way to buy a ticket — it tells you which date and route to point your search at.

Sequence: schedule gives you the plane and the date window, you search that route on those dates, you confirm on the seat map, you book.

Getting the price down

Compare day by day inside the window

Pull up a full month calendar view on the airline's own site. You want a day where the widebody is flying and the fare hasn't moved. On short repositioning hops the gap between a recliner and a flat bed is often a rounding error.

Short hops are the cheap way in

Two hours in an international business cabin costs a fraction of a transcon. Use the Under 3 hours filter. These are the flights where you pay domestic first-class money for a seat that cost the airline six figures.

Use distance-based award charts where they still exist

Partner programs that price by distance rather than demand are where short widebody hops get absurd. A sub-700-mile domestic first-class segment on a partner chart can cost a fraction of what the operating carrier's own dynamic pricing wants.

Check upgrades, not just fares

Confirmed-at-booking upgrades and elite upgrade instruments apply here like any domestic route. An upgrade clearing onto a widebody is worth several times one clearing onto a narrowbody.

Before you get attached to a flight

Equipment swaps are the main way this plan falls apart. Re-check the seat map about a week out and again the day before. You'll usually be re-seated automatically, but you want to know rather than find out at the gate.

And widebody days sell out earlier on routes where enthusiasts already know the schedule. If the plane is the reason for the trip, book early.
